DNA education hub

Understand the science before the result.

Clear, practical information about paternity testing, sample collection, interpretation and the limits of what a biological result can decide.

Family first

DNA answers a biological question. Families still need care, context and responsible decisions.

Understanding the science before testing helps participants know what the result can prove, what it cannot decide, and why identity and consent matter.

What is a DNA paternity test?

A DNA paternity test compares genetic markers from a child and an alleged father. Because a child inherits genetic material from biological parents, the pattern of markers can be evaluated to determine whether the tested man is consistent with being the biological father.

What sample is normally collected?

Many modern relationship tests use a buccal sample, collected by gently swabbing the inside of the cheek. It is non-invasive and usually quick to collect. The collection team may provide instructions about eating, drinking, smoking or other substances before collection so the sample is suitable for processing.

Why are identification and consent important?

Identity verification helps establish who actually provided each sample. Consent ensures that participants understand why the test is being done, how samples and personal information will be handled, and — in the Genesis programme — how filming and publication may occur.

How are results described?

Relationship-testing reports commonly distinguish between an exclusion and a very high probability of paternity based on the markers tested. The exact language and statistical presentation depend on the laboratory and test performed. Families should read the complete report rather than relying only on a headline or social-media summary.

A DNA result answers a biological relationship question. It does not automatically decide custody, guardianship, maintenance, inheritance, parental responsibility or other legal issues.

Can relatives be tested if a parent is unavailable?

Sometimes. Kinship testing can use biological relationships among available relatives to evaluate whether a claimed family relationship is supported. The strength and suitability of a kinship test depend on which relatives can participate and the purpose of the test.

Home testing versus documented testing

Testing arrangements can differ in how identities are verified, samples are witnessed and records are maintained. If a result may be needed for an official or legal purpose, families should ask the testing provider what documentation and chain-of-custody procedures are required before samples are collected.

What should families consider before testing?

  • What question are you trying to answer?
  • Who needs to participate for the test to be informative?
  • Does the result need to be suitable for an official or legal purpose?
  • How will adults communicate the result to a child, if a child is involved?
  • What support may be needed after the result?
  • Who will have access to the result and associated personal information?

DNA testing and family welfare

Scientific certainty can resolve one form of uncertainty while creating new emotional or practical questions. Responsible testing therefore includes thinking ahead about privacy, communication, child welfare and the consequences of either possible outcome.
